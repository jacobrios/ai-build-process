# Rule lineage

Append-only record of why each user-level rule exists and what produced it. Organized to mirror `~/.claude/CLAUDE.md`'s sections so a rule and its story are findable from each other. The rules file is operative and wins where the two disagree; this file exists so the rules file does not have to carry its own history.

How to extend: when a rule is born or amended, the rule gets a short dated marker in CLAUDE.md and the narrative lands here, in the matching section, the same session. Nothing here is ever rewritten; corrections are dated annotations.

Created 31 July 2026, during the length pass that followed the rules consolidation (see `2026-07-31-rules-consolidation.md`). The initial entries below reconstruct lineage that previously lived inline in CLAUDE.md; the consolidation record holds the full audit reasoning behind the 31 July changes and is not duplicated here, only summarized where a rule needs its story to stand alone.

This file is the living home for rule reasoning and lineage going forward, and it inherits from the consolidation record rather than competing with it: where the two describe the same decision, the consolidation record is the original account, sealed to its date, and this file carries anything learned since.

## The 28 August 2026 compression pass

Run after the file grew from about 25,900 to 31,995 characters in two weeks, entirely through the amendment loop, which only ever adds. Landed at 29,901: roughly 2,100 characters, 360 words, and two bullets, with no rule losing force. Method worth reusing: a fresh subagent read the file cold with no knowledge of which choices were deliberate, and a second, separate subagent adjudicated four disputes between the applying session and that cold read, seeing both positions unlabeled and in shuffled order.

The check earned itself. The applying session was overruled twice, once on something that would have quietly weakened a gate. It proposed collapsing the merge rule's repeated prohibitions as redundant; the judge showed they are two distinct requirements, "do not merge automatically" governing the agent's own action and "merging locally is not among the options offered" governing what a finishing flow presents to a tired human at the end of a build. Collapsing them would have deleted the half aimed at the Superpowers finish menu. The session was also overruled on moving the file's closing maintenance note ("a rule earns inline space by being unpredictable and cheap") to this record: the note passes its own test, and a session asked to shorten CLAUDE.md loads CLAUDE.md and has no reason to open this file first, the same reasoning that keeps spec anatomy inline rather than in a template. The cold read's own top-ranked finding, cutting all forty dated markers, was rejected: they are how a rule finds its entry here, and at about 1,000 characters they were never the large cut they were claimed to be. What was large was the narrative that had grown inside those parentheses, which is a different thing and did come out.

Two bullets merged. The debt-flagging rule folded into the findings-reach-me-translated rule, keeping the worked example ("not 'this violates separation of concerns,' but 'this will make it harder to add multi-venue support later'"), which the judge identified as the sharpest instance of the product-language standard anywhere in the file. The two repo-boundary bullets became one after the cold read caught that they granted write access anywhere and revoked it in the next line.

Corrected, not compressed: the top-of-file note claimed the product-language standard was "not restated per-section" while three later rules restated it. The claim was false, so the claim was fixed rather than the rules deleted; a file whose meta-claims about itself are unreliable teaches a session to distrust the rest.

Levers applied, from Matt Pocock's `writing-for-agents`: seven prohibitions restated positively where the positive carries identical force, on the principle that naming a forbidden behavior makes it more available rather than less; three leading words replacing definitions (a slice is *vertical*, exclusions are *non-goals*, the assistant is the *tech lead*); and war stories moved out of inline parentheses while the dated stamps stayed. Prohibitions kept as genuine guardrails: the em dash ban, the merge rule, and "show it works, do not say it works," which the cold read correctly called a memorable pair rather than a bare negation. Left alone by decision: the slice document's four locations, since each sits under its topically correct heading, this file mirrors those sections, and the word-target rule covers three artifacts at once and cannot be relocated intact.

## Core operating principles

**"I do not read code" (rewritten 31 July 2026).** Replaced "I (the product manager) read your output at roughly 30% technical comprehension. Flag what matters rather than expecting every diff to be read." The old line was fake precision on an undefined scale, and it described the wrong variable: it understated Jacob on reasoning and product content, where he is a competent and argumentative reader, and overstated him on artifacts, where he is a non-reader by deliberate choice, not a partial one. The parallel line in the claude.ai project instructions was replaced the same day. The final wording folded in a sentence from the applying session's draft (the "disclosure that lives only in the diff was never disclosed" framing).

## Before you build

**The design-gate exception (amended 31 July 2026).** The original wording was "a genuinely trivial change (a one-line copy tweak) needs a one-sentence note, not a walkthrough," a feel test. Superpowers' brainstorming skill explicitly rejects any such exception ("a todo list, a single-function utility, a config change" all go through design), which is the likely reason a small B1 Coach cold-start fix in late July 2026 felt heavier than it should have. The consolidation replaced the feel test with an objective fence plus a declared exception, accepting the residual risk of a session misjudging a change as behavior-free; the blast radius is limited because a skipped conversation never skips the PR. Dependency bumps were in the original skip list and were removed the same day, on the applying session's pushback: a session usually cannot verify that a bump changes no behavior, so listing bumps invited exactly the silent misjudgment the fence was built to prevent.

**One-shot risk absorbed into the slicing rule (31 July 2026).** "Flag one-shot risk on unproven ground" had stood alone in the engineering section since the file's first draft: if handed a large autonomous build touching integration points that don't yet exist or are untested, say so before starting and propose slicing the unproven parts out; on an established codebase with tested seams, proceed. After the length pass it was visibly derivable from the slicing rule, which already made proven ground the limit on slice size; two rules stating one principle at different moments invited confusion about whether they differed. Merged at Jacob's direction, keeping the one clause the slicing rule lacked (the same test applies when a big autonomous build arrives, since the risk won't always be visible from a product view).

**Coverage named in the slice document (added 28 August 2026).** From Matt Pocock's `tdd` skill (github.com/mattpocock/skills), which requires the seams under test to be written down and confirmed with the human before any test is written, on the grounds that nothing can be tested exhaustively so coverage is decided somewhere whether or not anyone says so. Adopted as a clause on the existing verification part of the slice document rather than as its own rule, for two reasons: a standalone confirm-first rule would add a third gate mid-execution, fighting the subagent-driven flow where the engine is told not to check in between tasks; and the slice document is already read and approved, so the decision arrives at a checkpoint that exists. Deliberately left out: the skill's other half, testing at public boundaries rather than internals, which is engineering technique Jacob would never decide and the model already does reasonably. The half worth having is "which deliberately do not," turning a silent coverage gap into a stated claim he can disagree with, the same move as the declared design-gate skip.

**Spec anatomy (added 31 July 2026).** Un-parked after the Superpowers inventory showed the plugin requires and self-reviews a spec but has nothing for fencing settled decisions, exclusions-with-homes, verification-written-before-code, or a debt forecast. It is a rule rather than a template because the plugin writes the spec and would never open a template file.

## What to surface, and when

**The execution-go gate (amended 31 July 2026).** The plugin has no plan gate: its `writing-plans` skill ends by asking "Which approach?", which is a choice of execution engine, not approval of the plan, and a casual "1" starts execution, after which the engine is instructed not to pause between tasks. On paper there was never a plan gate here either (plans are not surfaced by default), but the old fresh-session handoff was one in practice: a plan handed to a fresh session could not begin until Jacob pasted the prompt. Retiring that flow without a replacement would have deleted the gate silently. The resolution: an explicit go before any autonomous multi-task run, leaving exactly two gates in the flow, start of execution and merge, both Jacob's and both unmissable.

**Debt flagging.** The rule originally carried a second worked example ("this shortcut works now, but changing this feature after MVP will likely mean redoing this part"); trimmed in the length pass, one example carries the pattern.

**Findings arrive translated (added 3 Aug 2026).** Born the Monday after a weekend away, when a chain of three tooling micro-PRs in interplanetary-groups (#42, #43, #44) each surfaced new suggested work in its review, and Jacob named the real problem: product findings are instantly triageable, technical findings are not, so "safer to assume it's important" was the standing bias and fixing loops were eating days. The pre-AI ritual was to ask the engineering manager for a recommendation and mostly trust it; the rule makes that conversation mandatory for anything technical surfaced for a decision, from any source, not just slice reviews. Key reasoning from the discussion: a review chain never terminates on its own (every competent review finds something), so "done" is a decision, not a state the reviews reach; on a healthy team most logged findings are correctly never fixed, because a logged finding is cheap to defer and the only expensive findings are unlogged ones; and in a shop where every change costs a full PR with review and QA, "quick fix" is a fiction, which makes queue the rational default. The forced-decline clause exists because "decline is allowed" proved weaker than "decline is mandatory when nothing gets worse"; "unsure means queue, never yes" is the one clause aimed at Jacob rather than at the surfacer, countering his stated bias directly. Example whys (security risk, tiny job even for an edge case, big job for small risk) were deliberately kept out of the rule text at Jacob's call: examples in an always-loaded file act as templates and would pull future whys toward their shapes, so the texture lives here instead. A merge with the debt-flagging rule was drafted and rejected; debt-at-creation disclosure and finding-at-decision translation are two different moments, and Jacob wanted the simple "tell me when debt is created" line intact. First worked application, same day: PR #44's three open questions triaged to queue (path normalization plus making the QA script a permanent test, one combined micro-PR), decline (parsing Bash commands to close the shell bypass; real fragility cost, protecting only against a now-documented accident), and queue-low (sixteen test files sharing the 5-second remote-database timeout).

## Working from designs and source material

**Describe-what-you-see.** The rule's original inline example: "This mockup shows a two-line schedule row with a venue underneath. I don't see a field for group size anywhere, flagging that before I build." The founding failure it prevents is the silent-drop: a one-shot build of an interplanetary-groups mockup shipped everything with a data home and silently skipped a "beers once a month" row no field could hold (recorded in that project's CLAUDE.md and build-notes).

## Verify and report honestly

**Self-report and show-it-works (merged 31 July 2026).** These were two adjacent rules from the file's first draft; once their padding was stripped in the length pass they were saying one thing (evidence over assertion, with the obligation attaching to the claim) and were merged.

**Baseline ledger (added 31 July 2026).** From the repo audit. Superpowers' worktree skill runs the suite before work starts but records nothing, so nothing later distinguishes a pre-existing failure from a new one, and nothing requires naming carried failures in the PR. "The previous slice's finishing number lives in the project's decision record" was named explicitly the same day, on the applying session's pushback that the rule as drafted left the first session to apply it guessing where to look.

**Model behavior evidence (added 31 July 2026).** The strongest single finding of the repo audit, found independently by three of four auditors, and confirmed entirely absent from Superpowers, whose testing worldview is wholly deterministic. The working example is interplanetary-groups' `eval:detect` bench: twenty-two realistic cases graded through the real prompt, normalize layer, and reply ladder, deliberately outside the test suite because it costs money and hits the network; it took the recognition fix from 26/50 runs to 50/50 with the must-stay-quiet cases holding 45/45.

**The phone check narrowed to a two-level gate (amended 31 August 2026).** The rule had said "anything visual" gets a real-phone pass, which was broader than Jacob's actual practice, so it was either costing him setup time on screens that never needed it or being skipped by judgment while the file misdescribed his process. Surfaced as a side flag during the worktree amendment, when he explained why the phone is not always his QA surface, and fixed as its own change rather than folded into that one.

The gate is now two-level and checkable. First the project: mobile-first or not. B1 Coach was never mobile-first, so it never earns a phone pass, which the old rule wrongly demanded. Interplanetary-groups is. Then the change: the first pass on a new screen or layout, and any change that could alter vertical space. Later tweaks that cannot grow the page are a desktop-browser check. The judgment call narrows from "is this visual," which caught nearly everything, to "could this change vertical space," which is answerable about a diff.

Kept from the original: the LAN-address serving, folding findings into the checklist, and stopping the dev server afterward including a stray one. Moved here: the evidence that produced the rule on 14 August 2026, that twice a real-screen check produced findings a full desktop pass missed entirely, tests included, and the second time it reordered which slice came next. The worked example ("a card taller than the screen can carry") stayed inline deliberately, because it is what tells a session what "could alter vertical space" looks like rather than being decoration. Two clauses were cut in drafting, both restating what the rule already settled: Jacob caught that "a project that was never mobile-first never needs this" duplicates the bold header's own scope, and the applying session caught that "later tweaks are fine in a desktop browser" is the negation of "two triggers only." Net result is about 50 characters shorter than the rule it replaced.

**The QA script rule (added 28 July 2026; procedure moved to its own file 31 July 2026).** The script was requested after every PR until it became clear it should be the default: the checks a build agent cannot make (voice, feel, visual behavior) are exactly the ones a product manager can. Chat delivery was added 29 July 2026 because making Jacob open the PR to find his checklist is friction. The ready-to-run requirement was added 29 July 2026 after a QA round where the sandbox, the server, and the links each had to be requested separately, spending the five minutes of QA attention on fetching things rather than judging them. The strict-sequence requirement was added the same day, after a group-home link was opened before its join link and the QA stalled on a missing chat input that was never a bug: the checklist was right and stopped one step short. A handoff that lists two links and five steps reads as five steps but is actually a graph, and the product manager should never have to infer it. The full procedure now lives in `~/.claude/checklists/pr-handoff.md`.

**The dev server stopped being the agent's to start (amended 20 August 2026).** The ready-to-run
requirement above said "server running," and it was followed exactly: a server was started for a QA
handoff and left up. Over the next three hours the branch was switched under it about six times, and
Jacob's phone was left holding JS chunks that no longer existed on disk. Hydration silently failed, so
his typing showed in the fields while React never saw it, and the Continue button never enabled. He
reported it as a product bug, which is what it looked like. It cost a debugging cycle and one
confidently wrong diagnosis before the environment was suspected. The rule had been written to kill
friction (a handoff where the sandbox, the server, and the links each had to be requested separately)
and nobody had yet seen a long-lived server fabricate a symptom. Two things made the amendment safe
rather than a return to the chore list: a fenced `bash` block renders as a play button in the app, so
one tap is still turnkey, and seeded state does not rot the way a server does, so staging stays the
agent's job. A second, independent argument surfaced in the same session: Jacob's LAN address changed
mid-session when his Mac moved onto his phone's hotspot, so every URL already pasted went dead, while a
command he runs prints whatever the current network URL is.

## Communication style

**The three message-shape rules (added 11 August 2026).** Born from Jacob's feedback after ten days of running the consolidated rules: messages were text-heavy, and actions or steps sometimes arrived buried in paragraphs, including a QA list delivered as prose. Inspired by the i-have-adhd skill (github.com/ayghri/i-have-adhd) but deliberately not installed: always-on communication behavior belongs in the always-loaded rules file, not an invoked skill, and several of its ten rules conflict with standing obligations (suppress-tangents against flag-debt, spot-and-say-so, and propose-next-slice; no-recap against the evidence rules). Three of its ideas survived in our own words. The tangent rule marks rather than suppresses because Jacob values the asides sometimes and skips them other times, so the design goal was making skipping cost one glance, not removing the content; mid-message placement was allowed at his direction since an aside can be worth more in context, and the filter-decides-whether clause was added to keep the marking from becoming a loophole past the surfacing filter. The list rule generalizes what the PR handoff file already required for QA scripts, after the "seven rules, and why" paragraph in the very message proposing the rule proved the point.

**The two length rules and the tail reminder (added 12 August 2026).** From Anthropic's Opus 5 prompting guidance, which says the model's responses and its written files both run longer than prior models and that effort settings do not shorten visible output: length has to be asked for explicitly. This closed a real gap. The 11 August rules govern message shape and the core filter governs whether something is said at all, but nothing governed how many words the things that passed took, which is why Jacob's wordiness complaint survived the August rules. The written-document rule is two-sided ("cover the substance, but do not pad") because a purely negative rule would license thin decision records, which are the more expensive failure; that clause is the guidance's own wording, kept after Jacob asked whether the source text beat the paraphrase. The rest was written in the file's voice rather than pasted, since the guidance frames its blocks as examples and its "keep responses focused" clause overlaps the lead-with-what-I-need rule. The tail line follows the guidance's advice to pair a conciseness instruction with a short reminder at the end of a long prompt; (not 100% sure, verify) the guidance shows it wrapped in an XML tag, and the assumption here is that position rather than the tag is what does the work.

Same review pass, no action taken, recorded so it is not re-derived: Superpowers' `verification-before-completion` is exactly the "legacy harness scaffolding" the guidance says to remove, since Opus 5 self-verifies natively, but it stays because a confident unverifiable claim is the most expensive failure in this setup and disabling one skill would mean forking the plugin. The investigate-via-subagent rule may now over-delegate against the guidance's "do not delegate what you can finish in a handful of tool calls," and is being watched rather than edited. Same-session subagent execution, the independent reviewer, and the report-everything-then-filter review convention were all checked against the guidance and are aligned, not in conflict.

## Engineering process and safety nets

**The reviewer is a standing instruction (amended 25 August 2026).** Sessions began stopping to ask "run the reviewer?" before opening a PR, twice in two days, because they were started with guidance not to spawn agents unless asked and correctly refused to pick silently between that and this rule. Diagnosis, with its own correction: auto mode was blamed first and is not the cause, since the permission-modes documentation shows auto mode supports subagents with a three-point classifier review (task description before spawn, every action during the run, full history on return) rather than discouraging them; the instruction is not in any file under `~/.claude` or in b1-coach's settings either. (Not 100% sure, verify) the likely source is harness-level delegation guidance, which would match Anthropic's Opus 5 page recommending harnesses cap subagent spawning because the model delegates readily and small-task delegation multiplies cost. The amendment makes the rule its own standing ask, which resolves the collision whatever layer generates the instruction. Worth recording for the next time this comparison comes up: the delegation best practice is written for engineers who read their own diffs, where a reviewer subagent competes with their own eyes; here it is the only window into the code, so it is a disclosure obligation rather than a cost optimization. The per-task implementer pattern and this reviewer are both cases the same Anthropic guidance endorses (genuinely independent tracks, and writer-verifier patterns); its warning against subagents double-checking "your own work" does not reach a reviewer checking a different agent's output.

**Planning context never writes the code (rewritten 31 July 2026).** The original pair of rules (plan to disk then execute in a fresh session, added after the interplanetary-groups spark slice; end planning with a paste-able handoff prompt, added 30 July 2026) encoded one fresh session per slice. The spark slice evidence (build-notes §11, 23 July 2026: the fresh execution session caught its own weak test and a missing case that a review pass then also caught independently) proved fresh context catches what polluted context misses, but not that one fresh session was the right implementation. Superpowers' subagent-driven engine produces more fresh contexts (one per task), plus an independent reviewer per task, crash recovery via an on-disk ledger, and per-task model cost control, so it satisfies the principle better; the fresh-session flow was demoted to the fallback for plugin-free projects.

**Same-session subagent execution named as the default (amended 11 August 2026).** In the ten days after the 31 July rewrite, planning sessions ended by handing Jacob a paste-able carryover prompt instead of offering to execute, three times. The rule's own headline taught the misreading: "the context that plans never writes the code" let a session conclude it was disqualified from executing, when the implementer subagents are the context that writes code and the session merely coordinates; meanwhile the fallback's concrete procedure was the most actionable sentence in the bullet, so sessions kept reaching for it. The sessions were following the file's accidental override of the plugin, whose own default (subagent-driven-development) was already same-session. The amendment names same-session execution as the default in the headline, fences the handoff to plugin-free projects only, and channels the long-context worry into a declared deviation (the session recommends the fallback, Jacob accepts or declines) rather than a silent reason to hand off. The engine stays described in one clause rather than only named by skill, per the 31 July decision that this file outlives any plugin.

**One slice document, and a PR body written for a stranger (amended 12 August 2026).** Measured while investigating the slowdown: each slice was producing a spec of 1,600 to 2,200 words, a plan of 5,700 to 9,900, a decision-record entry, and a PR body, four documents describing one slice. Jacob's own account is what settled the shape: he approves the spec unread, because he has just had the whole conversation and trusts the write-up; he does not open the PR at all; and he values the long plan precisely because its detail is what lets a cheaper model execute reliably. So the cut went to the document with no reader (the spec becomes a 200-word front section on the plan) and to the parts of the PR written for him (the QA script and open questions move to chat, where he uses them). The PR keeps a verification section, over his suggestion to cut further, because its audience is an interviewer skimming the repo and specific falsifiable numbers are the most credible anti-slop signal available; length was the problem, not the section. Worth recording for the next time this stack grows: nobody designed these four documents as a set, they accumulated one rule at a time, and each was individually reasonable. Also worth recording, because it came up three times in one evening: the hook rule, the remote test database, and this document stack were all build-agent suggestions that Jacob adopted on trust, which is the argument for auditing suggested process periodically rather than only when it hurts.

**The test gate split in two (amended 12 August 2026).** The original rule, "automated hooks run tests after every file edit," was written when the interplanetary-groups suite was small and cheap. By 12 August it was 84 files, network-bound against a remote database, and 83 seconds; Jacob reported build runs going from ninety minutes to two-to-four hours and asked whether a process change had caused it. The transcripts settled it: per-edit round trip 9-15 seconds in late July, 85 seconds on 12 August, 5.6 hours of one day spent waiting on edits. Two hypotheses were rejected with evidence before any fix was proposed, and one of them was Jacob's own (same-session execution; coordinator context was flat at 259-296k tokens across three weeks, and slice size showed no trend), which is why the measurement mattered more than the change did. The rule was rewritten rather than dropped, because what it protected is intact: a mechanical gate nobody has to remember. Narrow tests per edit, whole suite when a task's agent finishes. Jacob pulled the read-only-agent optimization forward from "later, if it bites" on hearing it was ten lines and failed safe. The amendment insists on both halves together, since the per-edit run alone is a weaker gate than the rule ever meant. Two further findings from the same PR, both recorded in the template README rather than here: this directory's own working-directory fix had never been carried back into the project it came from, and the QA script handed over with the PR reproduced the exact filter-that-silently-drops-a-line defect the handoff checklist was written to prevent, for the third time.

**Worktrees allowed, and slices may run concurrent (amended 31 August 2026).** Supersedes the two entries below and the attention argument in the slice-discipline entry; both stay as written, per append-only. Jacob asked for the worktree ban to be tested rather than assumed, and a session tested it on interplanetary-groups.

What was believed: that a worktree is a checkout that quietly misbehaves, with no database, nothing for `db:which` to read, and a dev server showing old code, all of it landing on the browser QA gate. What the test showed: a bare worktree does fail, but **loudly and in the first thirty seconds**, with `npm run db:which` refusing outright and naming all three missing values. That is the opposite of a silent failure, and it is the specific claim the ban rested on. With the environment file and the dependency directory symlinked in, everything worked: the right database, the suite running, typecheck clean, and the worktree on `main` while the primary checkout stayed on a feature branch, both live, neither disturbing the other's uncommitted edits.

The limitation that survived, and the reason the amended rule is not simply "worktrees are fine": a symlinked dependency directory is shared between checkouts, so anything generated or installed into it is shared too. The sharpest case is the database client generated from the schema, so two branches that both change the schema must not run concurrent on symlinked dependencies. The applying session generalized it: two branches needing different packages hit the same trap by a different route, since one can import what only the other installed and its tests then pass for the wrong reason, which is precisely the failure a suite cannot catch.

On the slice rule, three drafts and two corrections from Jacob, both worth keeping. First: the old rule's stated constraint was his attention, and he declined to have his attention live in the file at all, because the honest answer varies week to week and belongs in the moment rather than in a standing rule. The applying session tried to keep the attention reasoning as the justification for the serial default; Jacob cut it on the grounds that it states a fact and names no action, which is the two-file division working correctly, so it lives here instead. Second, and sharper: the draft said "concurrency is my call," and he rejected it because noticing the opportunity requires judging file-disjointness, which is technical. Leaving him to spot it means it never gets spotted. The obligation is therefore on the session to raise it unprompted, the same shape as the propose-next-slice rule, with the decision his.

The QA clause went through three versions as Jacob questioned it. "Browser QA does not parallelize" was imprecise: two browsers were never the constraint. "QA runs on one phone" was also wrong, because he only takes the phone route when vertical space or real-screen rendering is genuinely in question, and checks simple screens like sign-in or error states in a desktop browser. The surviving mechanism is the shared database: symlinking the environment file points both checkouts at the same dev-test database, so a second QA pass mutates the state the first one's checklist expects, and the result reads as a product bug rather than a process collision. The rule stays conservative (no overlapping QA passes at all) rather than permissive (overlap when the data is unrelated), because judging "unrelated data" correctly is exactly the call Jacob cannot verify.

Flagged during this change, not acted on: the phone-check rule says "anything visual" gets a real-phone pass, which is broader than Jacob's actual practice as described above. Either it is being followed and wasting his time, or it is being skipped and the file misdescribes his process. Its own amendment.

**No worktrees (added 31 July 2026).** The plugin's `using-git-worktrees` creates a second checkout at `.worktrees/<branch>/`, git-ignored, runs `npm install` there, and says nothing about `.env`; git only carries tracked files, so the fresh worktree has no database connection, `db:which` has nothing to read, and a dev server run from the open folder shows old code. The skill honors a declared preference silently, which is why the preference is declared here. The concurrent micro-PR is the one case where isolation would earn its cost; the preference stands anyway because concurrent work is rare and the `.env` gap makes worktrees actively worse today.

**Superpowers standing state (added 31 July 2026).** Recorded because the enablement is invisible from inside any repo: B1 Coach acquired a design-before-code gate in June 2026 without a single file in the repo changing, purely because the machine-wide master switch was on.

**Specs and plans never on main (amended 31 July 2026).** The original rule kept plans untracked while planning was in flux, committed them on the slice branch when work began. Two things changed it. The tidiness argument (arguments should leave no trace) weakened when interplanetary-groups moved to true merge commits around PR 35 and began deliberately preserving intermediate history; and the audit verified the real risk was sequencing, not tracking: the plugin commits the spec the moment brainstorming ends, on whatever branch is checked out, so the protection is cutting the slice branch before design work begins, which was already happening in practice. The never-delete and never-plan-only-PR clauses date to 30 July 2026, when a plan was committed and then deleted in one project while a standalone plan-only PR was opened and withdrawn in the same session.

**The backup rule (added 30 July 2026).** Written when the `~/.claude` backup repo was created. It stays at full length inline because it passes the inline test: nothing at session start predicts that this directory will be edited, and the rule is four lines.

**Review before merge, proven in the PR body (rewritten 31 July 2026).** The original rule said a read-only subagent reviews code before it is committed. The audit found "before commit" was defending the wrong variable: what matters is whether review happened and whether Jacob was told, and there is no automatic review anywhere in the stack (the plugin's discipline is prose; Jacob's hooks run tests and block paths; Claude Code's own automatic review pass was reportedly removed in v2.1.215, not independently verified). ## Slice and branch discipline

**One slice at a time, and the micro-PR exception (amended 31 July 2026).** The original bullet's two sentences said different things ("one slice lands fully before the next opens" is absolute; "avoid concurrent open PRs touching the same files" is about overlap) with no why stated, so a session picked blind. Naming the binding constraint as Jacob's attention settled it, and the micro-PR exception (audit item 8) falls out: a micro-PR costs almost no attention and may run concurrent when disjoint; a second full slice may not, however disjoint.

**Deploy-time obligations (added 31 July 2026).** Audit item 10. The only coverage anywhere in the stack was a single reviewer-template question about migration strategy, which is a question a reviewer may raise, not a tracked obligation.

**Propose-next-slice (added 30 July 2026; compressed 31 July 2026).** The full original rationale: this is the "recommendation, not a menu" rule applied, not an exception to it; options show the shape of the choice, the recommendation makes them useful. The reasoning can be product-shaped (what a user or the business feels next) or engineering-shaped (building this first makes the others cheaper, unblocks them, or stops something being built twice), and the sequencing argument is the one a product manager is least able to see from his own seat, so volunteering it is exactly what a good engineering manager does. The moment a slice lands is when the most is known about what should follow, and that knowledge decays fast; leaving the product manager to reconstruct it weeks later costs him the best information he will ever have. Draw candidates from what the finished slice surfaced, the project's decision record, and the debt named along the way. The choice stays his.

**Open a PR and stop, with no local-merge option (amended 31 July 2026).** Superpowers' `finishing-a-development-branch` presents a fixed four-option menu where option 1 is merge locally. The menu asks, so nothing merges without a yes, but the failure mode is a tired human typing "1" at the end of a long build, which responds to the option not being offered, not to better instructions. A local merge skips the PR and with it the QA script, the decision-record entry, and the PR body disclosures at once. The asymmetry that clinched it: option 4 (discard) requires typing the word, while option 1, the one that violates the process, requires one character; the menu protects against data loss and not process loss, which is backwards here, since a discarded branch is far easier to recover than an unreviewed merge is to undo. Whether the finishing skill honors a declared preference the way the worktree skill does was not verified; a stated rule is sufficient grounds to skip the option and say why.

## Verify and report honestly: the mobile pass (added 14 August 2026)

The rule: anything visual gets a pass on a real phone before the QA script is written.

**What produced it.** The chat-feed-boundary slice (interplanetary-groups, PR #66) was verified
thoroughly on a desktop browser: rendered comparison against the design boards, computed styles
sampled from the live DOM, hit tests, 888 passing tests. All of it correct, and all of it blind
to the finding that mattered most. The owner ran the QA on a phone over the LAN and immediately
saw that the pinned card region had squeezed the chat feed into a strip, which no desktop
viewport showed because there was enough height for both. That finding reordered the slice
queue: card-height reduction jumped ahead of the next planned polish slice.

**Why it became a rule rather than a note.** It was the second occurrence. The first was the
enlarged-device-text check during the visual-language phase, which produced the "layout grows
with content, never clips" rule for the same underlying reason: the product is a phone product,
and desktop verification is a proxy that silently passes things the real target fails. A
recurrence is the project's own threshold for writing the solution down instead of solving it
again.

**Placed in "Verify and report honestly" rather than in the PR handoff checklist**, and
deliberately: it is an obligation on the verification itself, not on the handoff. Doing it only
at handoff time would mean discovering a layout problem after the branch is finished, which is
exactly what happened here. Its position directly under the rendered-comparison rule is the
point: a rendered comparison on the wrong screen size is still not a rendered comparison.
